How a Notion budget works under the hood

A spreadsheet budget uses SUMIF. Notion does the same job with three building blocks:

  • Two databases. One for categories (Rent, Groceries, Transport) with a Planned number, one for transactions with an Amount.
  • A relation. Each transaction links to one category. This is the Notion version of "which column does this go in".
  • A rollup and a formula. The category sums the Amount of its linked transactions (Spent). A formula then gives Remaining = Planned − Spent, and a status like if(prop("Remaining") < 0, "Over", "OK").
Example: Groceries planned €420. Four supermarket trips add up to €198.70, so Remaining shows €221.30 and the used bar sits at 47%. Fun & hobbies planned €60, spent €72: Remaining is −12 and the status turns red.

One page per month or one long list?

Keep one transactions database for the whole year and add a Month property (the templates fill it from the date). Then filter or group by month. Duplicating the page every month also works, but your history ends up split across 12 pages.

What Notion can't do (and what to do instead)

  • No bank sync. You type transactions in. Two minutes after each payday is enough for most people.
  • Formulas can't look across months on their own. For a yearly summary you need one more database that links to each month.

Questions

Is there a free budget template for Notion?

Yes. The Monthly Budget Planner and the Simple ADHD-friendly Budget on this page are free and work on the free Notion plan. You duplicate them into your own workspace in one click.

Can Notion connect to my bank account?

Not by itself. You enter transactions by hand, or use a separate automation tool. Most people log spending once or twice a week.

Does a Notion budget work in euros, pounds or dollars?

Yes. Number properties can use any currency format. Change the format on the Amount and Planned columns to your currency.

Notion or a spreadsheet for budgeting?

Notion is better if you already plan your life there and like linked pages and checklists. A spreadsheet is better for heavy maths, charts and long history.
